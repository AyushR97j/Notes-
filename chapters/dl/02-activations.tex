\chapter{Activations, losses and output layers}
\label{ch:activations}

Three choices sit at fixed places in every network: the non-linearity between
layers, the function that turns the last layer's numbers into a prediction, and
the loss that scores that prediction. OAs test all three with one-minute numerics
(``compute the softmax of these five logits'') and with classification questions
(``which of these is a loss and which is an activation?''). Interviews test whether
you know why the standard pairings are standard.

\section{Activation functions}
\prototype{Sigmoid, $\tanh$, ReLU and GELU evaluated at $z=-2$ give four different
answers, and the derivative of the sigmoid is never larger than
$\gv{c02_activations/dsig_max}$.}

\subsection{What the activation is for}
\Cref{ch:perceptron} showed that without an activation a deep network collapses to
one affine map. The activation is the \emph{only} source of non-linearity in an
MLP: weights and biases enter linearly, and the loss is applied after the network,
so it cannot change what functions the network represents.

\begin{remark}
	\trap ``Which component provides non-linearity: weights, biases, activation
	function, loss function?'' Only the activation function. A non-linear
	\emph{loss} (cross-entropy is very non-linear) does not help: a linear model
	trained with cross-entropy is still logistic regression, with a linear boundary.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Sigmoid and tanh: saturation}
Both squash $\RR$ into a bounded interval, and both are nearly flat away from
$0$: $\sigma'(5)=\gv{c02_activations/dsig_5}$. Backpropagation multiplies one such
factor per layer (\cref{ch:backprop}), and since $\sigma'\le\frac14$ ten sigmoid
layers shrink a gradient by at least $4^{10}$, i.e.\ to at most
$\gv{c02_activations/dsig_pow10}$ of its size from that factor alone. This is the
\vocab{vanishing gradient} problem in its simplest form.

The sigmoid has a second defect: its outputs are all positive. For a neuron fed by
sigmoid units, $\partial\Loss/\partial w_i = \delta\cdot h_i$ with every $h_i>0$, so
all of that neuron's weight gradients share the sign of $\delta$ and the weights can
only move together up or together down, giving zig-zag updates. $\tanh$ is
zero-centred and its maximum slope is $1$, so it was preferred for hidden layers
even before ReLU. Today sigmoid is used at outputs (probabilities) and in gates
(LSTM, \cref{ch:rnn}), almost never in hidden layers.

\subsection{ReLU and its relatives}
ReLU does not saturate for $z>0$ (derivative exactly $1$), is cheap, and produces
exact zeros (sparse activations). Its failure mode is the \vocab{dead ReLU}: a unit
whose pre-activation is negative on every training input gets zero gradient forever
(\cref{ch:backprop} has a simulation). Leaky ReLU and PReLU (learned $\alpha$) keep
a small slope for $z<0$; ELU makes the negative side smooth and pushes mean
activations toward zero.

\begin{remark}
	\trap ``ReLU is not differentiable at $0$, so it cannot be trained by gradient
	descent.'' The kink is a single point that floating-point inputs essentially
	never hit exactly; frameworks use the subgradient $0$ there, and ReLU is the
	default hidden activation of most CNNs.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Smooth modern activations: GELU and SiLU}
GELU weights the input by the probability that a standard normal falls below it,
$z\,\Phi(z)$; Transformers (BERT, GPT-style models) use it or SiLU in their
feed-forward blocks \cite{gelu}. The common approximation
$\tfrac12 z\bigl(1+\tanh(\sqrt{2/\pi}(z+0.044715z^3))\bigr)$ is within
$\gv{c02_activations/gelu_approx_err}$ of the exact GELU on $[-6,6]$. Both are
\emph{non-monotone}: SiLU has a minimum $\gv{c02_activations/silu_min}$ at
$z=\gv{c02_activations/silu_argmin}$, GELU a minimum $\gv{c02_activations/gelu_min}$
at $z=\gv{c02_activations/gelu_argmin}$ (found numerically). They behave like ReLU
for large $|z|$ but pass small negative signals and have a continuous derivative.

\begin{moral}
	Hidden layers: ReLU for CNNs and MLPs, GELU/SiLU for Transformers, $\tanh$ and
	sigmoid only inside recurrent cells and gates. Output layers: chosen by the task,
	next section.
\end{moral}

\section{Output layers: sigmoid and softmax}
\prototype{Logits $\z=(2,1,0.1,-1,0.5)$ for five classes; the softmax gives class
$1$ probability $\gv{c02_activations/z5_p0}$.}

\subsection{Softmax}
For $K$ classes the network outputs \vocab{logits} $\z\in\RR^K$ and
\[
	\softmax(\z)_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j}}.
\]
It is positive and sums to $1$, preserves the order of the logits, and is invariant
to adding a constant to every logit (multiply top and bottom by $e^{-c}$), but not to
scaling them.

\begin{example}[Softmax confidence from five logits]
	\label{ex:softmax5}
	For $\z=(2,1,0.1,-1,0.5)$: $e^{z}=(\gv{c02_activations/z5_e0},
	\gv{c02_activations/z5_e1}, \gv{c02_activations/z5_e2}, \gv{c02_activations/z5_e3},
	\gv{c02_activations/z5_e4})$ with sum $\gv{c02_activations/z5_sum}$, so
	\[ \softmax(\z) = (\gv{c02_activations/z5_p0},\ \gv{c02_activations/z5_p1},\
	\gv{c02_activations/z5_p2},\ \gv{c02_activations/z5_p3},\ \gv{c02_activations/z5_p4}). \]
	The model predicts class 1 with confidence $\gv{c02_activations/z5_conf}\%$.
	Note that a logit gap of $1$ ($2$ vs $1$) is a probability ratio of $e\approx2.72$,
	not a difference of $1$ in anything.
\end{example}

\subsection{Overflow and the log-sum-exp trick}
$e^{1000}$ overflows double precision, so the textbook formula applied to
$\z=(1000,1001,1002)$ returns $\gv{c02_activations/naive_nan}$ for every entry. The fix
uses shift invariance: subtract $m=\max_k z_k$ first. Then every exponent is
$\le0$, the largest term is exactly $1$, and nothing overflows. The same trick gives a
stable \vocab{log-sum-exp}
\[
	\LSE(\z)=\log\sum_k e^{z_k} = m + \log\sum_k e^{z_k-m},
\]
which is what a cross-entropy implementation actually needs.

For $(1000,1001,1002)$ the stable version returns

identical to $\softmax(0,1,2)$, and $\LSE=\gv{c02_activations/lse_big}$; both

\subsection{Sigmoid is two-class softmax}
$\softmax(a,b)_1 = e^a/(e^a+e^b) = 1/(1+e^{-(a-b)}) = \sigma(a-b)$ (checked
numerically). So a binary classifier needs one logit and a sigmoid; two logits and a
softmax is equivalent but over-parameterised.

\subsection{The softmax Jacobian}
With $\bm p=\softmax(\z)$, $\partial p_i/\partial z_j = p_i(\delta_{ij}-p_j)$, i.e.\
$J = \diag(\bm p)-\bm p\bm p\T$ (verified against \pt{torch.autograd.functional.jacobian}).
Rows sum to zero because the outputs always sum to $1$. In practice you never form $J$:
combined with cross-entropy it collapses to the gradient $\bm p-\y$ derived below.

\begin{remark}
	\trap Multi-label is not multi-class. If an image can contain both a cat and a
	dog, use $K$ independent sigmoids with $K$ binary cross-entropies. A softmax forces
	the probabilities to compete and sum to $1$, so ``cat $0.9$, dog $0.8$'' is
	impossible under it.
\end{remark}

\section{Loss functions}
\prototype{True class 1 for the logits above: cross-entropy
$-\ln \gv{c02_activations/z5_p0} = \gv{c02_activations/z5_ce0}$ nats.}

\subsection{Cross-entropy is maximum likelihood}
If the network outputs a distribution $\bm p_\theta(\cdot\mid\x)$ over $K$ classes and
the data are independent, the negative log-likelihood of the labels is
\[
	-\sum_{i}\log p_\theta(y_i\mid\x_i)
	= \sum_i \Bigl(\LSE(\z_i) - z_{i,y_i}\Bigr),
\]
the \vocab{categorical cross-entropy} between the one-hot label and $\bm p$.
Minimising it is minimising the KL divergence from the empirical label distribution
to the model (the information-theory section of the companion book
\otherbook{Classical Machine Learning} has the identity
$H(q,p)=H(q)+\KL(q\Vert p)$).

\begin{proposition}[Gradient of softmax + cross-entropy]
	For one example with logits $\z$, one-hot label $\y$ and
	$\Loss=-\sum_k y_k\log\softmax(\z)_k$,
	\[ \pd{\Loss}{\z} = \softmax(\z)-\y. \]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	$\Loss = \LSE(\z)-\z\T\y$ because $\sum_k y_k=1$. The gradient of $\LSE$ is the
	softmax, and the gradient of $\z\T\y$ is $\y$.
\end{proof}

For \cref{ex:softmax5} with true class 1 the gradient, identical to autograd's, is
\[ \pd{\Loss}{\z} = (\gv{c02_activations/z5_g0},\ \gv{c02_activations/z5_g1},\
\gv{c02_activations/z5_g2},\ \gv{c02_activations/z5_g3},\ \gv{c02_activations/z5_g4}). \]
The gradient pushes the true logit up by $1-p_y$ and each wrong logit down by its
probability: big mistakes get big corrections, and the gradient never saturates
while the prediction is wrong.

\begin{remark}
	\trap Sanity check at initialisation: with small random weights the logits are
	nearly equal, so the loss of a $K$-class classifier starts near $\ln K$. For a
	batch of $64$ random $10$-class logits of scale $0.01$ the script measures
	$\gv{c02_activations/ce_init}$ against $\ln10=\gv{c02_activations/ln10}$. A first
	loss far from $\ln K$ means a bug (wrong labels, softmax applied twice, a huge
	initial scale).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Binary cross-entropy and its stable form}
For $y\in\{0,1\}$ and $p=\sigma(z)$, $\mathrm{BCE}=-[y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)]$:
$\gv{c02_activations/bce_09}$ for $p=0.9$, $y=1$; $\gv{c02_activations/bce_01}$ for
$p=0.1$, $y=1$; $\ln2=\gv{c02_activations/bce_05}$ at $p=0.5$; and
$\gv{c02_activations/bce_099}$ for a $0.99$ prediction of the wrong class. Computing
$\sigma$ first and then $\log$ breaks for large $|z|$: at $z=-1000$, $y=1$ the
sigmoid underflows to $0$ and the loss becomes $\gv{c02_activations/naive_bce_m1000}$.
Working from the logit,
\[
	\mathrm{BCE}(z,y) = \max(z,0) - zy + \log\bigl(1+e^{-|z|}\bigr),
\]
gives the correct $\gv{c02_activations/stable_bce_m1000}$. This is
\pt{BCEWithLogitsLoss}, and the reason you pass logits, not probabilities, to it.

\subsection{Why not MSE for classification?}
With a sigmoid output and squared error $\frac12(\sigma(z)-y)^2$, the gradient is
$(\sigma(z)-y)\,\sigma'(z)$. When the prediction is confidently wrong ($y=1$,
$z=-5$) the factor $\sigma'(z)$ kills it: $\gv{c02_activations/gmse_m5}$ for MSE
against $\gv{c02_activations/gce_m5}$ for cross-entropy. The model that is most
wrong learns slowest under MSE. Cross-entropy's $\log$ exactly cancels the
sigmoid's saturation. (MSE on a sigmoid is also non-convex in $z$; CE is convex.)

\subsection{Margin losses: hinge}
With labels $y\in\{-1,+1\}$ and score $z$, every binary loss is a function of the
\vocab{margin} $m=yz$. The 0--1 loss $\ind{m\le0}$ is what we care about but has zero
gradient almost everywhere; the \vocab{hinge} $\max(0,1-m)$ (SVMs) and the logistic loss
$\log(1+e^{-m})$ are convex upper bounds (the logistic in base 2 so that it passes
through $(0,1)$). The multiclass hinge sums
$\max(0,z_j-z_y+1)$ over wrong classes $j$; for \cref{ex:softmax5} with true class 2
it is $\gv{c02_activations/hinge5}$ (\pt{F.multi\_margin\_loss} divides by $K$).

\subsection{Regression: MSE, MAE, Huber}
MSE is the Gaussian negative log-likelihood and is dominated by outliers; MAE
(Laplace likelihood) is robust but has a constant-size gradient that does not shrink
near the optimum. \vocab{Huber} interpolates: quadratic within $\delta$, linear
outside.

With $\delta=1$, a residual of $10$ costs $\gv{c02_activations/huber_10}$ under Huber
and $\gv{c02_activations/mse_10}$ under $\frac12r^2$; a residual of $0.2$ costs
$\gv{c02_activations/huber_02}$ under both (matches \pt{F.huber\_loss}).

vastly outnumbers the rest:

\section\problemhead

\begin{problem}\onechili
	A 4-class classifier outputs logits $(1,1,1,1)$. What is the cross-entropy
	loss? What if the logits are $(101,101,101,101)$?
	\begin{hint}Shift invariance.\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		Both give the uniform distribution, so the loss is $\ln4\approx\gv{c02_activations/ln4}$
		(row 2 of the table).
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\onechili
	Show $\tanh z=2\sigma(2z)-1$ and deduce $\tanh'(0)=4\sigma'(0)$.
	\begin{hint}Write $\tanh z=(1-e^{-2z})/(1+e^{-2z})$.\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		$2\sigma(2z)-1 = \frac{2}{1+e^{-2z}}-1=\frac{1-e^{-2z}}{1+e^{-2z}}=\tanh z$.
		Differentiating, $\tanh'(z)=4\sigma'(2z)$, so $\tanh'(0)=4\cdot\frac14=1$.
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\twochili
	Derive $\partial\,\mathrm{BCE}/\partial z = \sigma(z)-y$ for $\mathrm{BCE}=
	-[y\log\sigma(z)+(1-y)\log(1-\sigma(z))]$, and verify that the stable form
	$\max(z,0)-zy+\log(1+e^{-|z|})$ equals BCE for both signs of $z$.
	\begin{hint}$\log\sigma(z)=-\log(1+e^{-z})$ and $\log(1-\sigma(z))=-z-\log(1+e^{-z})$.\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		Using the hint, $\mathrm{BCE}=\log(1+e^{-z})+(1-y)z$. Its derivative is
		$-\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}+1-y = -(1-\sigma(z))+1-y=\sigma(z)-y$. For $z\ge0$ the
		stable form is $z-zy+\log(1+e^{-z})$, the same expression. For $z<0$ write
		$\log(1+e^{-z})=-z+\log(1+e^{z})$; then
		$\mathrm{BCE}=-zy+\log(1+e^{z})=0-zy+\log(1+e^{-|z|})$.
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\twochili
	A softmax classifier predicts $p_y=0.2$ for the true class. By how much must the true
	logit increase, other logits fixed, to halve the loss? Give a formula, then evaluate
	for $p_y=0.2$.
	\begin{hint}Let $S$ be the sum of the other exponentials; $p_y=e^{z_y}/(e^{z_y}+S)$.\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		Halving $-\ln p_y$ means the new probability is $\sqrt{p_y}$. With
		$p=e^{z}/(e^z+S)$, the odds $p/(1-p)=e^z/S$, so increasing $z$ by $\Delta$
		multiplies the odds by $e^\Delta$:
		$\Delta = \ln\frac{\sqrt{p}/(1-\sqrt{p})}{p/(1-p)}$. For $p=0.2$:
		$\sqrt{0.2}=\gv{c02_activations/pd_sqrt}$, odds $\gv{c02_activations/pd_odds_new}$ vs
		$\gv{c02_activations/pd_odds_old}$, so $\Delta=\ln\gv{c02_activations/pd_ratio}\approx
		\gv{c02_activations/pd_delta}$ (the script checks that the loss indeed halves).
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\onechili
	A team trains an image tagger where each image may have several of 20 tags, with a
	softmax output and categorical cross-entropy. What goes wrong, and what is the fix?
	\begin{hint}What does a softmax force the 20 probabilities to do?\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		The softmax makes the tags compete: probabilities sum to $1$, so an image with
		four true tags can give each at most about $0.25$, and thresholds become
		meaningless. Use 20 sigmoid outputs with a binary cross-entropy per tag
		(\pt{BCEWithLogitsLoss} on 20 logits).
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}
