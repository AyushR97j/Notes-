\chapter{Language models and LLMs}
\label{ch:llm}

A language model assigns probabilities to sequences of tokens; a large language model is a
Transformer trained to do that on a very large corpus and then adapted to follow
instructions. Placement OAs mostly ask what fine-tuning means; interviews at AI companies
ask how tokenisation works, how decoding knobs change outputs, what LoRA saves, how to
serve a model in less memory, and how retrieval reduces hallucination. This chapter covers
each at the depth of one good interview answer, with the arithmetic done by code.

\section{Tokens and embeddings}
\prototype{Byte-pair encoding learns that \texttt{e}+\texttt{p} is the most frequent pair in a
small corpus and merges it first.}

Models see integer \vocab{tokens}, not characters or words. Word-level vocabularies cannot
handle unseen words; character-level sequences are long. \vocab{Subword} tokenisers sit in
between: frequent words become one token, rare words split into pieces.
\vocab{Byte-pair encoding} (BPE) \cite{bpe} starts from characters (with an end-of-word marker)
and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair:

\codefile[\script{c09_llm}]{gen/dl/snip/c09_llm-bpe.py}

\begin{example}[BPE on a toy corpus]
	Word counts: \pt{deep} 6, \pt{deeper} 3, \pt{deepest} 2, \pt{keep} 4, \pt{keeper} 2, \pt{sleep} 3. The
	first eight merges (pair count in brackets, \pt{\_} marks end of word) are
	\gv{c09_llm/bpe_merges}. The corpus shrinks from $\gv{c09_llm/bpe_before}$ symbols to
	$\gv{c09_llm/bpe_after}$. Unseen words are still encodable: \pt{sleeper} $\to$
	\pt{\gv{c09_llm/enc_sleeper}}, \pt{keepest} $\to$ \pt{\gv{c09_llm/enc_keepest}}. The script checks
	that decoding inverts encoding and that encoding each training word reproduces its training
	segmentation.
\end{example}
Production tokenisers work on bytes (so any string is encodable), learn tens of thousands to a few
hundred thousand merges, and (WordPiece, unigram LM) differ in how they choose merges. Consequences
worth saying in an interview: costs and context limits are counted in tokens; numbers and rare scripts
fragment into many tokens; spelling-level tasks are hard because the model never sees letters.

An \vocab{embedding} layer is a lookup table $\mathbf E\in\RR^{V\times d}$: token $i$ becomes row $i$.
It is equivalent to multiplying a one-hot vector by $\mathbf E$, and it is trained like any other
weight. Similar tokens end up with similar vectors because they appear in similar contexts
(\cref{ch:generative} derives word2vec).

\section{Objectives and model families}
\prototype{GPT predicts the next token; BERT fills in masked tokens; T5 maps an input text to an output
text.}

\begin{itemize}
	\ii \vocab{Causal (autoregressive) LM}: $p(x_1,\dots,x_T)=\prod_tp(x_t\mid x_{<t})$, trained with
	cross-entropy on every position at once under a causal mask. Decoder-only (GPT family). Natural for
	generation.
	\ii \vocab{Masked LM} (BERT \cite{bert}): choose $15\%$ of positions; of those, replace $80\%$ by a
	\pt{[MASK]} token, $10\%$ by a random token, keep $10\%$; predict the originals using context on both
	sides. Simulated on $100{,}000$ tokens: $\gv{c09_llm/mlm_sel}\%$ selected, $\gv{c09_llm/mlm_mask}\%$
	masked, $\gv{c09_llm/mlm_rand}\%$ random, $\gv{c09_llm/mlm_keep}\%$ kept. The random and kept cases stop
	the model from learning that only \pt{[MASK]} positions matter. Encoder-only; strong representations for
	classification and retrieval; not a generator.
	\ii \vocab{Sequence-to-sequence} (T5, BART): an encoder reads the input, a decoder generates the output;
	pretrained by reconstructing corrupted spans.
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}
	The \vocab{perplexity} of a model on a sequence of $T$ tokens is
	\[ \PPL=\exp\Bigl(-\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\log p(x_t\mid x_{<t})\Bigr), \]
	the exponential of the per-token cross-entropy: the effective number of equally likely choices per token.
\end{definition}
For per-token probabilities $0.5,0.25,0.1,0.4,0.8$ the mean NLL is $\gv{c09_llm/ppl_ce}$ nats and
$\PPL=\gv{c09_llm/ppl}$ (the inverse geometric mean of the probabilities, as checked). A uniform model over
$V$ tokens has $\PPL=V$ (checked for $V=\gv{c09_llm/ppl_uniform}$). Perplexities are only comparable with
the same tokeniser and test text.

\section{Scale}
\prototype{Training a $1$B-parameter model on $20$B tokens costs about $6ND=\gv{c09_llm/c_flops}$ FLOPs.}

Training one token through a dense Transformer costs about $2N$ FLOPs forward and $4N$ backward, so total
training compute is $C\approx6ND$ for $N$ parameters and $D$ tokens. \vocab{Scaling laws} are empirical
power laws: in the regimes studied, test loss falls smoothly as a power of $N$, $D$ and $C$ when the other
two are not bottlenecks \cite{kaplan}. A later analysis concluded that compute-optimal training scales $N$
and $D$ in roughly equal proportion, which in their fits came to roughly twenty tokens per parameter
\cite{chinchilla}; many later models train on far more tokens than that because inference cost favours
smaller models. At a sustained $40\%$ of $100$ TFLOP/s, the $1$B/$20$B example above is about
$\gv{c09_llm/c_days}$ GPU-days. Treat all such exponents and constants as properties of particular fits, not
laws of nature.

\section{Adapting a pretrained model}
\prototype{LoRA with rank $8$ on one $4096\times4096$ weight trains $\gv{c09_llm/lora_8}$ parameters instead of
$\gv{c09_llm/lora_full}$ ($\gv{c09_llm/lora_pct_8}\%$).}

\subsection{Fine-tuning, from cheapest to most expensive}
\begin{itemize}
	\ii \vocab{Prompting} / in-context learning: no weights change; instructions and examples go in the input.
	\ii \vocab{Feature extraction} (linear probing): freeze the model, train a small head on its embeddings.
	\ii \vocab{Prompt / prefix tuning}: learn a few virtual-token embeddings (or per-layer key/value prefixes)
	prepended to the input; the model stays frozen.
	\ii \vocab{Adapters}: small bottleneck MLPs inserted in each layer and trained, model frozen.
	\ii \vocab{LoRA} \cite{lora}: below.
	\ii \vocab{Full fine-tuning}: update every weight on the task data, with a small learning rate. Best
	quality per example when data are plentiful, but one full copy of the model per task, and optimiser state
	(\cref{ch:pytorch}) for every parameter.
\end{itemize}
\vocab{Instruction tuning} is supervised fine-tuning on (instruction, good response) pairs, turning a
next-token predictor into an assistant. \vocab{RLHF} \cite{instructgpt} then collects human preferences
between pairs of responses, trains a reward model on them, and optimises the policy against that reward with
reinforcement learning (typically PPO) plus a KL penalty that keeps it close to the supervised model.
\vocab{DPO} \cite{dpo} reaches the same kind of objective without a separate reward model or RL loop: a
classification-style loss directly on preference pairs that raises the log-probability ratio (relative to a
frozen reference model) of preferred over rejected responses.

\subsection{LoRA}
Freeze the pretrained $\W_0\in\RR^{d_\text{out}\times d_\text{in}}$ and learn a low-rank update:
\[ \W=\W_0+\tfrac{\alpha}{r}\,\B\A,\qquad \B\in\RR^{d_\text{out}\times r},\ \A\in\RR^{r\times d_\text{in}},\ r\ll d. \]
$\B$ starts at zero so training starts exactly from the pretrained model; after training $\B\A$ can be merged
into $\W_0$, so inference costs nothing extra.

\codefile[\script{c09_llm}]{gen/dl/snip/c09_llm-lora.py}

The script checks that the layer equals the base layer at initialisation, that merging gives the same output,
and that the trainable count is $r(d_\text{in}+d_\text{out})$.
\begin{example}[What LoRA saves]
	For a $4096\times4096$ matrix: rank $4$, $8$, $16$, $64$ train $\gv{c09_llm/lora_4}$, $\gv{c09_llm/lora_8}$,
	$\gv{c09_llm/lora_16}$, $\gv{c09_llm/lora_64}$ parameters, i.e.\ $\gv{c09_llm/lora_pct_4}\%$,
	$\gv{c09_llm/lora_pct_8}\%$, $\gv{c09_llm/lora_pct_16}\%$, $\gv{c09_llm/lora_pct_64}\%$ of the full matrix.
	For an illustrative 32-layer model with $\dmodel=4096$ ($\approx12L\dmodel^2=\gv{c09_llm/lora_model_full}$
	weights in its blocks), rank-8 LoRA on $\Wq$ and $\Wv$ only trains $\gv{c09_llm/lora_model_lora}$
	($\gv{c09_llm/lora_model_pct}\%$). The saving is in gradients and optimiser state (and in storing one small
	adapter per task); the frozen weights still have to fit in memory for the forward pass.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
	\trap ``LoRA reduces inference latency'' or ``LoRA makes the model smaller''. It reduces the number of
	\emph{trainable} parameters and the training memory; after merging, the model has exactly the original size
	and speed. Combined with a quantised frozen base (QLoRA-style) it also cuts the memory of the frozen weights.
\end{remark}

\section{Decoding}
\prototype{Logits $(3,2.5,1,0.5,0,-1)$: greedy always says token 1; top-$p$ with $p=0.9$ samples among
$\gv{c09_llm/dec_p09_n}$ tokens.}

Given next-token logits, \vocab{greedy} decoding takes the arg-max, \vocab{beam search} keeps $k$ hypotheses
(\cref{ch:rnn}), and sampling methods reshape the distribution before drawing:

\codefile[\script{c09_llm}]{gen/dl/snip/c09_llm-sampling.py}

\begin{example}[Four decoding distributions]
	For $\z=(3,2.5,1,0.5,0,-1)$:
	\begin{center}
		\small
		\begin{tabular}{ll}
			\toprule
			rule & distribution over the six tokens \\
			\midrule
			softmax ($T=1$) & $(\gv{c09_llm/dec_p})$ \\
			$T=0.5$ & $(\gv{c09_llm/dec_t05})$ \\
			$T=2$ & $(\gv{c09_llm/dec_t2})$ \\
			top-$k$, $k=2$ & $(\gv{c09_llm/dec_k2})$ \\
			top-$p$, $p=0.9$ & $(\gv{c09_llm/dec_p09})$ \\
			\bottomrule
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	Cumulative sorted probabilities are $(\gv{c09_llm/dec_cum})$, so the nucleus for $p=0.9$ is the first three
	tokens, renormalised (checked against a brute-force implementation).
\end{example}
Temperature $\to0$ recovers greedy; top-$k$ uses a fixed number of candidates even when the distribution is
flat or peaked, which top-$p$ adapts to. Greedy and beam search tend to produce repetitive, generic text in
open-ended generation; sampling with moderate temperature and top-$p$ is the usual default there, while
greedy is preferred for tasks with one right answer (code, extraction).

\section{Evaluation and its limits}
\prototype{``the cat is on the mat'' against the reference ``the cat sat on the mat'': BLEU-2
$\gv{c09_llm/bleu2}$.}

\vocab{BLEU} (translation) multiplies a brevity penalty by the geometric mean of clipped $n$-gram precisions
of the output against references; \vocab{ROUGE} (summarisation) emphasises recall, e.g.\ ROUGE-L uses the
longest common subsequence. In the example, unigram precision $\gv{c09_llm/bleu_p1}$, bigram precision
$\gv{c09_llm/bleu_p2}$, brevity penalty $\gv{c09_llm/bleu_bp}$, BLEU-2 $\gv{c09_llm/bleu2}$; the LCS has length
$\gv{c09_llm/rouge_lcs}$, so ROUGE-L recall and precision are both $\gv{c09_llm/rouge_r}$. The limits: a correct
paraphrase (``a cat was sitting on the mat'') gets bigram precision only $\gv{c09_llm/bleu_para_p2}$; one wrong
word can flip meaning at a small score cost; and none of these measures factuality. Perplexity measures
fit to text, not usefulness. LLMs are therefore evaluated on task benchmarks, by human preference, and
increasingly by other models as judges, each with its own biases (contamination of benchmarks by training
data being the main one).

\section{Making models cheaper: quantisation, distillation, MoE}
\prototype{A $7$B-parameter model needs $\gv{c09_llm/mem_fp16}$\,GB for its weights in fp16 and
$\gv{c09_llm/mem_int4}$\,GB in 4-bit.}

\subsection{Quantisation}
Weights alone: $\gv{c09_llm/mem_fp32}$\,GB in fp32, $\gv{c09_llm/mem_fp16}$ in fp16/bf16,
$\gv{c09_llm/mem_int8}$ in int8, $\gv{c09_llm/mem_int4}$ in 4-bit, for $7\times10^9$ parameters. Symmetric
per-tensor int8:
\codefile[\script{c09_llm}]{gen/dl/snip/c09_llm-quant.py}
For a $64\times64$ Gaussian matrix (std $0.05$) with one outlier of $0.4$ the scale is $\gv{c09_llm/q_scale}$ and the
worst error $\gv{c09_llm/q_err}$ (codes identical to \pt{torch.quantize\_per\_tensor}); without the outlier the
worst error is $\gv{c09_llm/q_err_noout}$, and the mean error with it is $\gv{c09_llm/q_ratio}\times$ larger. One
outlier stretches the grid for everyone, which is why practical schemes use per-channel or per-group scales
and treat outliers separately. \vocab{Post-training quantisation} needs no retraining;
\vocab{quantisation-aware training} simulates rounding during training.

\subsection{Distillation}
A small \vocab{student} is trained to match a large \vocab{teacher}'s softened output distribution:
$\Loss=T^2\,\KL\bigl(\softmax(\z_t/T)\Vert\softmax(\z_s/T)\bigr)$, usually mixed with the ordinary
label loss. Temperature reveals the teacher's ``dark knowledge'': for teacher logits $(4,1.5,1,-1)$ the
$T=1$ distribution is $(\gv{c09_llm/kd_pt1})$ but at $T=2$ it is $(\gv{c09_llm/kd_pt})$, exposing which wrong
classes are nearly right. With student logits $(3,2,0,-0.5)$ the loss is $\gv{c09_llm/kd}$ (equal to
\pt{F.kl\_div}); the $T^2$ keeps gradient magnitudes comparable across temperatures.

\subsection{Mixture of experts}
An MoE layer replaces the FFN by $E$ expert FFNs and a router that sends each token to the top $k$. For an
illustrative layer with $\dmodel=4096$, a gated FFN of width $14{,}336$ ($\gv{c09_llm/moe_ffn}$ parameters)
and $E=8$, $k=2$: $\gv{c09_llm/moe_total}$ parameters stored, $\gv{c09_llm/moe_active}$ ($\gv{c09_llm/moe_frac}\%$)
active per token. Capacity grows with total parameters while compute per token follows active parameters;
the costs are memory for all experts, routing load balance (auxiliary losses), and communication when experts
live on different devices.

\begin{remark}
	\trap ``An MoE model with $X$ total parameters runs as fast as a dense model with $X$ parameters'' (it runs
	closer to the speed of its \emph{active} size, if memory bandwidth allows) and ``quantisation reduces FLOPs''
	(it mainly reduces memory and memory traffic; speed-ups need kernels for the low-precision format).
\end{remark}

\section{Retrieval, tools and failure modes}
\prototype{A question about the KV cache retrieves the document about the KV cache, which is pasted into the
prompt before the model answers.}

\vocab{Retrieval-augmented generation} (RAG): embed documents into vectors offline; at query time embed the
question, retrieve the top-$k$ most similar chunks (cosine similarity, approximate nearest-neighbour index),
and put them in the prompt with an instruction to answer from them and cite them. In a four-document toy with
TF-IDF vectors the query ``what does the KV cache store during decoding?'' retrieves document
$\gv{c09_llm/rag_best}$ (cosine $\gv{c09_llm/rag_score}$, matching scikit-learn); real systems use learned dense
embeddings, chunking, re-ranking and hybrid keyword search. RAG gives the model fresh and private knowledge
without retraining and makes answers checkable. \vocab{Tool use} and \vocab{agents} extend the idea: the model
emits structured calls (search, code execution, APIs), the system runs them and feeds results back, looping
until done.

\vocab{Hallucination}: fluent, confident, wrong output, a consequence of training to produce likely text rather
than true text. Mitigations: grounding with retrieval and citations, asking the model to abstain when the
context lacks the answer, lower temperature for factual tasks, verification steps and tools, evaluation sets
that measure it. Safety basics an interviewer expects: prompt injection (instructions hidden in retrieved or
user content), data leakage of private information, harmful-content filtering, bias, and the principle that a
model's output is untrusted input to the rest of the system.

\begin{moral}
	Pretrain to predict tokens, adapt cheaply (LoRA, instruction tuning, preference optimisation), decode with
	the right knob, serve with fewer bits, and ground answers in retrieved evidence.
\end{moral}

\section\problemhead

\begin{problem}\onechili
	A language model assigns probabilities $0.2$, $0.5$ and $0.1$ to the three tokens of a test sentence. What is its
	perplexity on the sentence?
	\begin{hint}$\exp$ of the mean negative log-probability.\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}NLLs $(\gv{c09_llm/pa_nll})$, mean $\gv{c09_llm/pa_mean}$, so
		$\PPL=e^{\gv{c09_llm/pa_mean}}=(0.2\cdot0.5\cdot0.1)^{-1/3}=\gv{c09_llm/pa_ppl}$.\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\onechili
	How many trainable parameters does LoRA with $r=16$ add to a $1024\times4096$ projection, and what fraction of the
	matrix is that?
	\begin{hint}$r(d_\text{in}+d_\text{out})$.\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}$16\cdot(1024+4096)=\gv{c09_llm/pb_n}$, i.e.\ $\gv{c09_llm/pb_pct}\%$ of the $4{,}194{,}304$
		weights.\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\twochili
	Logits $(2,1,0)$. Give the sampling distribution for temperature $0.5$, for top-$k$ with $k=1$, and for top-$p$ with
	$p=0.5$. Which of them are deterministic?
	\begin{hint}Top-$p$ keeps the smallest prefix with mass $\ge p$.\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		With $T=0.5$ the logits become $(4,2,0)$ and the distribution is $(\gv{c09_llm/pc_t05})$.
		With $k=1$: $(1,0,0)$. Top-$p$:
		$\softmax(2,1,0)=(\gv{c09_llm/pc_p})$; the first token alone has mass $\ge0.5$, so
		$(\gv{c09_llm/pc_topp})$. Top-$k$ with $k=1$ and this top-$p$ are deterministic
		(greedy).
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\onechili
	A team fine-tunes a 3B-parameter model by full fine-tuning in fp16 with Adam, and runs out of memory. Give two
	changes from this chapter and what each saves.
	\begin{hint}Where does training memory go?\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		LoRA: gradients and Adam moments only for the low-rank matrices (well under $1\%$ of parameters), instead of
		for all 3B. Quantise the frozen base (e.g.\ 4-bit), cutting weight memory about four-fold from fp16. Gradient
		checkpointing (\cref{ch:backprop}) additionally cuts activation memory.
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\twochili
	Explain why BERT cannot simply be used for left-to-right text generation, and why GPT is a weaker sentence encoder
	for retrieval than a bidirectional encoder of the same size.
	\begin{hint}What can each position see during pretraining?\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		BERT was trained to fill masked tokens from both sides and never to model $p(x_t\mid x_{<t})$; it has no causal
		structure for sampling one token at a time. GPT's representation of a token sees only the left context, so early
		tokens of a sentence carry little information about the rest; a bidirectional encoder's representations use the
		whole sentence.
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}
