\chapter{Optimisation for ML}
\label{ch:optim}
Every model in this book is fitted by minimising something. This short chapter collects the facts about
\emph{how} that you need for follow-up questions: why gradient descent zig-zags, what momentum buys, when Newton
or L-BFGS is the right tool, and why SGD needs a decaying step. Deep-learning optimisers (Adam and friends) are
covered in \otherbook{Deep Learning}.

\section{Gradient descent on a quadratic}
\prototype{Minimise $\frac12(x^2+10y^2)$ starting from $(1,1)$.}

Near a minimum every smooth loss looks quadratic, $f(\x)=\frac12\x\T\A\x$ with $\A\succ0$ having eigenvalues
$\mu=\lambda_{\min}\le\dots\le L=\lambda_{\max}$. Gradient descent $\x\leftarrow\x-\eta\A\x$ multiplies the
component along eigenvector $i$ by $1-\eta\lambda_i$ each step (\Cref{ch:linreg}, Problem 3F). Hence:
\begin{itemize}
	\ii it converges iff $0<\eta<2/L$; at $\eta=0.21>2/L=\gv{c16_optim/eta_max}$ the iterate's norm after 20 steps
	is $\gv{c16_optim/div_norm}$ and growing;
	\ii the best constant step $\eta=2/(\mu+L)$ contracts the error by $\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}$ per step, where
	$\kappa=L/\mu$ is the \vocab{condition number} (the script confirms the ratio $9/11$ for $\kappa=10$);
	\ii the number of iterations to a fixed accuracy grows linearly in $\kappa$.
\end{itemize}
Geometrically, with $\kappa\gg1$ the level sets are long thin ellipses; the gradient points across the valley,
not along it, so the iterates bounce between the walls (\Cref{fig:zigzag}). Standardising features and
preconditioning reduce $\kappa$.

\section{Momentum}
\prototype{A ball rolling down the valley keeps its speed along the floor and damps the side-to-side bounce.}
\vocab{Heavy-ball momentum} keeps a velocity: $\vv\leftarrow\beta\vv-\eta\nabla f(\x)$, $\x\leftarrow\x+\vv$.
Oscillating gradient components cancel in the running sum; consistent ones accumulate. With optimally tuned
$\eta,\beta$ on a quadratic the contraction factor improves from $\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}$ to
$\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}$: iterations scale with $\sqrt\kappa$ instead of $\kappa$. Iterations to reach
$\norm{\x}<10^{-6}$ from $(1,1)$:

$\sqrt\kappa$ rate for general smooth convex functions.

\section{Newton and quasi-Newton}
\prototype{One Newton step solves a quadratic exactly.}
Newton's method uses curvature: $\x\leftarrow\x-\Hm\inv\nabla f$. On a quadratic it jumps to the minimiser in one
step (checked); near a minimum of a smooth function it converges \emph{quadratically} (the number of correct
digits doubles each step), and it is invariant to linear rescaling of the parameters, so conditioning does not
matter. Costs: forming and solving with the $d\times d$ Hessian, $O(d^3)$ per step; and far from the minimum, or
where $\Hm$ is not PD, it can go the wrong way --- hence damping and line searches. Logistic regression's IRLS
(\Cref{ch:logistic}) is Newton's method.

\vocab{Quasi-Newton} methods build a Hessian (inverse) approximation from successive gradient differences: BFGS
keeps a dense $d\times d$ matrix; \vocab{L-BFGS} keeps only the last $m$ (say 10) pairs and costs $O(md)$ per
step --- the default solver for scikit-learn's logistic regression. On the Rosenbrock function (a curved,
ill-conditioned valley) from $(-1.2,1)$, plain gradient descent with step $10^{-3}$ needs
$\gv{c16_optim/ros_gd}$ iterations to get the gradient norm below $10^{-5}$; BFGS needs $\gv{c16_optim/ros_BFGS}$,
L-BFGS $\gv{c16_optim/ros_LBFGSB}$ and Newton-CG $\gv{c16_optim/ros_NewtonCG}$.

\section{Coordinate descent, SGD and duality}
\prototype{One data point per update: fast, noisy, and never quite settles at a constant step.}
\vocab{Coordinate descent} minimises over one coordinate at a time; it shines when each one-dimensional problem
has a closed form, as for the lasso (\Cref{ch:biasvar}) and SMO's two-variable steps (\Cref{ch:svm}).

\vocab{Stochastic gradient descent} replaces $\nabla f$ by the gradient on one example or a mini-batch: an
unbiased but noisy estimate, $n$ times cheaper per step. With a constant step, the iterates reach a noise ball
around the optimum whose size is proportional to $\eta$; convergence requires a decaying step
($\sum\eta_t=\infty$, $\sum\eta_t^2<\infty$, e.g.\ $\eta_t\propto1/t$) or averaging of iterates.

Linear regression with 2000 rows: the constant step plateaus at excess MSE $\approx\gv{c16_optim/sgd_const_end}$;
the decaying step keeps improving ($\gv{c16_optim/sgd_decay_end}$). Rates to remember for convex problems: GD on
smooth convex $O(1/t)$, on strongly convex $O(c^t)$ (linear); SGD $O(1/\sqrt t)$ and $O(1/t)$ respectively.

\vocab{Duality} (\Cref{ch:maths}) turns a constrained primal into a dual whose value lower-bounds it; for convex
problems with Slater's condition they coincide. Practical uses: the SVM dual exposes kernels and sparsity; the
\emph{duality gap} is a certificate of how far an iterate is from optimal, which is exactly the stopping test
scikit-learn's lasso and SVM solvers use.

\begin{moral}
	Condition number sets gradient descent's speed; momentum turns $\kappa$ into $\sqrt\kappa$; curvature (Newton,
	L-BFGS) removes it at a price per step; SGD trades exactness for cheap steps and needs a decaying rate.
\end{moral}

\section\problemhead

\begin{problem}[Largest step]
	\onechili
	For $f(x,y)=2x^2+8y^2$, what is the largest constant learning rate for which gradient descent converges, and
	what is the condition number?
	\begin{hint}
		The Hessian is $\diag(4,16)$.
	\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		$L=16$, $\mu=4$: converge iff $\eta<2/16=0.125$; $\kappa=4$, and the best constant step $2/(4+16)=0.1$
		contracts errors by $(4-1)/(4+1)=0.6$ per step.
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Newton in one dimension]
	\twochili
	Apply two Newton steps to minimise $f(x)=x^4-3x$ from $x_0=1$.
	\begin{hint}
		$x\leftarrow x-f'(x)/f''(x)$ with $f'=4x^3-3$, $f''=12x^2$.
	\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		$x_1=1-1/12=\gv{c16_problems/p16b_x1}$; $x_2=\gv{c16_problems/p16b_x2}$; the minimiser is
		$(3/4)^{1/3}=\gv{c16_problems/p16b_star}$ --- two steps already give four correct digits.
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}
