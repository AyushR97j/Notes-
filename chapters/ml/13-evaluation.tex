\chapter{Evaluation and Model Selection}
\label{ch:eval}
Most failed ML projects do not fail at modelling; they fail at measurement --- the wrong metric, a leaky
split, a test set consulted twenty times. Interviewers know this, so expect: precision and recall from a
confusion matrix in one minute, ROC AUC by hand, ``accuracy is 97\%, is the model good?'', and ``how did
you choose the hyper-parameters without fooling yourself?''

\section{The confusion matrix}
\prototype{1000 transactions, 60 frauds; the model flags 50, of which 40 are fraud.}

\begin{center}
	\begin{tabular}{cc|cc}
		& & \multicolumn{2}{c}{predicted} \\
		& & positive & negative \\ \hline
		\multirow{2}{*}{actual} & positive & TP $=40$ & FN $=20$ \\
		& negative & FP $=10$ & TN $=930$
	\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{center}
	\small
	\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{l l c >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X}
		\toprule
		metric & formula & value & question it answers \\ \midrule
		accuracy & $\frac{TP+TN}{\text{all}}$ & \gv{c13_eval/acc} & fraction correct \\
		precision (PPV) & $\frac{TP}{TP+FP}$ & \gv{c13_eval/P} & of the flagged, how many are fraud? \\
		recall (sensitivity, TPR) & $\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$ & \gv{c13_eval/R} & of the frauds, how many did we catch? \\
		specificity (TNR) & $\frac{TN}{TN+FP}$ & \gv{c13_eval/spec} & of the genuine, how many passed? \\
		FPR & $\frac{FP}{FP+TN}$ & \gv{c13_eval/fpr} & false-alarm rate \\
		F1 & $\frac{2PR}{P+R}$ & \gv{c13_eval/F1} & harmonic mean of $P$ and $R$ \\
		$F_\beta$ & $\frac{(1+\beta^2)PR}{\beta^2P+R}$ & $F_2=\gv{c13_eval/F2}$ & recall weighted $\beta$ times as much \\
		balanced accuracy & $\frac{TPR+TNR}{2}$ & \gv{c13_eval/bal} & accuracy with equal class weight \\
		MCC & $\frac{TP\cdot TN-FP\cdot FN}{\sqrt{\cdots}}$ & \gv{c13_eval/mcc} & correlation of predictions and labels \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
(All values checked against \code{sklearn.metrics}; $F_{0.5}=\gv{c13_eval/F05}$ favours precision.)
A model predicting ``not fraud'' for everyone scores accuracy $\gv{c13_eval/always0_acc}$ --- almost as
good --- with recall $0$. With imbalanced classes accuracy is nearly meaningless.

\begin{remark}
	\trap Which to optimise is a business decision: recall when misses are expensive (cancer screening, fraud),
	precision when false alarms are expensive (spam filtering into a hidden folder, recommending a loan
	rejection). Precision depends on the prevalence; recall and specificity do not. A model with the same
	TPR/FPR deployed where fraud is ten times rarer has much lower precision (recall the base-rate example of
	\Cref{ch:maths}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Macro, micro, weighted}
For $K$ classes compute per-class precision/recall/F1 one-vs-rest, then average:
\vocab{macro} (plain mean: every class equal), \vocab{weighted} (mean weighted by class support),
\vocab{micro} (pool all TP/FP/FN first: for single-label multiclass, micro-F1 $=$ accuracy).
\begin{example}[Three classes]
	Confusion matrix (rows true) $\begin{psmallmatrix}50&3&2\\10&25&5\\2&3&10\end{psmallmatrix}$. Per-class
	F1: $(\gv{c13_eval/mc_f1})$. Macro-F1 $=\gv{c13_eval/macro}$, weighted $=\gv{c13_eval/weighted}$,
	micro $=$ accuracy $=85/110=\gv{c13_eval/micro}$. The small third class drags macro-F1 down; use macro when
	minority classes matter.
\end{example}

\section{ROC and PR curves}
\prototype{Eight scored examples, swept threshold by threshold.}

A scoring classifier gives a curve, not a point. Sweeping the threshold from high to low traces the
\vocab{ROC curve} (TPR against FPR) and the \vocab{precision--recall curve}.
\begin{example}[ROC by hand]
	Scores $0.95,0.85,0.80,0.70,0.55,0.45,0.40,0.20$ with labels $1,1,0,1,0,1,0,0$ (4 positives, 4 negatives).
	Predicting positive for score $\ge t$:
	\begin{center}
		\small
		\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
			\toprule
			threshold $t$ & label at $t$ & TPR & FPR & precision \\ \midrule
			\gv{c13_eval/roc_rows}
			\bottomrule
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	Each positive moves the curve up by $1/4$, each negative right by $1/4$. Area by trapezoids:
	$\gv{c13_eval/auc}$. Average precision (mean of the precision at each positive's rank) $=
		(1+1+0.75+0.667)/4=\gv{c13_eval/ap}$. Both match scikit-learn.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[AUC is a ranking probability]
	$\AUC=\Prob(\text{score of a random positive}>\text{score of a random negative})$, counting ties as
	$\frac12$ (the normalised Mann--Whitney $U$ statistic).
\end{theorem}
Above, $\gv{c13_eval/auc_pairs}$ of the $\gv{c13_eval/n_pairs}$ positive--negative pairs are correctly ordered:
$13/16=0.8125$. Consequences: AUC is threshold-free and invariant to any monotone transformation of the
scores (so it says nothing about calibration); $0.5$ is random ranking; below $0.5$ means the ranking is
inverted.

\begin{remark}
	\trap ``AUC 0.9, so the model is great.'' With 1\% positives, the same model has average precision
	$\gv{c13_eval/imb_ap}$: FPR is tiny relative to the huge negative class, but in absolute numbers those false
	positives swamp the true ones. For rare positives, report PR-AUC (average precision) and precision at the
	operating recall.
\end{remark}

\section{Regression and probabilistic metrics}
\prototype{Five house-price predictions scored five ways.}
For $y=(3,5,2.5,7,10)$ and $\hat y=(2.5,5,4,8,9)$: MAE $=\gv{c13_eval/mae}$, MSE $=\gv{c13_eval/mse}$, RMSE
$=\gv{c13_eval/rmse}$ (same units as $y$), MAPE $=\gv{c13_eval/mape}\%$, $R^2=\gv{c13_eval/r2}$. MSE punishes
large errors (and is minimised by the mean), MAE treats all errors linearly (minimised by the median), MAPE is
undefined at $y=0$ and penalises over-prediction of small values asymmetrically; RMSLE suits targets spanning
orders of magnitude.

Probabilistic classifiers should also be judged as probabilities. \vocab{Log-loss} $-\frac1n\sum\log\hat p_{y_i}$
and the \vocab{Brier score} $\frac1n\sum(\hat p_i-y_i)^2$ are \emph{proper} scoring rules: they are minimised in
expectation by the true probabilities. On the imbalanced data the logistic model has log-loss
$\gv{c13_eval/imb_ll}$ versus $\gv{c13_eval/imb_ll_const}$ for always predicting the base rate.

\section{Cross-validation}
\prototype{Five folds, each used once for validation; but what if the rows are days?}

\vocab{$k$-fold CV}: split into $k$ folds, train on $k-1$, validate on the remaining one, average. $k=5$ or $10$
is the usual compromise: \vocab{leave-one-out} ($k=n$) is nearly unbiased but has high variance and costs $n$
fits (except for models with closed-form LOO, like linear smoothers). Variants:
\begin{itemize}
	\ii \textbf{Stratified} $k$-fold keeps class proportions in each fold (default for classification).
	\ii \textbf{Group} $k$-fold keeps all rows of a group (patient, user) in one fold.
	\ii \textbf{Time-series} (forward-chaining) splits train on the past and validate on the future, with an
	optional gap.
	\ii \textbf{Repeated} CV averages over several random partitions to reduce variance.
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}[Shuffled CV on a time series]
	A trending series, random-forest regressor on the time index. Shuffled 5-fold CV reports MAE
	$\gv{c13_eval/ts_random}$: each validation day is surrounded by training days, so the model interpolates.
	Forward-chaining CV reports $\gv{c13_eval/ts_forward}$: the model must extrapolate the trend, which a forest
	cannot do. The second number is what production will see.
\end{example}

\section{Leakage}
\prototype{5000 pure-noise features, 60 rows, ``92\% cross-validated accuracy''.}
\vocab{Leakage} is information in training that will not be available at prediction time. A catalogue:
\begin{enumerate}
	\ii \textbf{Preprocessing on all data before splitting}: scaling, imputation, PCA, feature selection,
	target encoding, SMOTE.
	\ii \textbf{Target leakage}: a feature computed after the outcome (``refund issued'' to predict fraud; ``days
	in hospital'' to predict admission).
	\ii \textbf{Temporal leakage}: random splits of time-ordered data; features using future aggregates.
	\ii \textbf{Group leakage}: the same entity in train and test.
	\ii \textbf{Duplicates} or near-duplicates across the split.
	\ii \textbf{Tuning on the test set} (\Cref{ch:setup}).
\end{enumerate}
\begin{example}[Feature selection outside the loop]
	60 rows, 5000 features of pure noise, random labels. Selecting the 20 features most correlated with the
	labels on \emph{all} the data and then cross-validating a logistic regression gives accuracy
	$\gv{c13_eval/leak_acc}$. Putting the selection inside a \code{Pipeline}, refitted within each fold, gives
	$\gv{c13_eval/proper_acc}$ --- chance, the truth.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
	\trap ``My validation score is much better than expected'' is a leakage alarm, not a celebration. The
	standard defence is a \code{Pipeline} (every fitted transform lives inside the CV loop) plus a split that
	mirrors deployment (by time, by group).
\end{remark}

\section{AIC, BIC and hyper-parameter search}
\prototype{Choose a polynomial degree for 60 points without a validation set.}

\subsection{Information criteria}
With $\hat L$ the maximised likelihood, $k$ the number of free parameters and $n$ the sample size:
\[ \mathrm{AIC}=2k-2\ln\hat L,\qquad \mathrm{BIC}=k\ln n-2\ln\hat L. \]
Lower is better. Both penalise parameters; BIC's penalty $\ln n$ exceeds AIC's $2$ once $n\ge8$, so BIC picks
smaller models. AIC \cite{akaike1974} estimates expected predictive (KL) loss; BIC \cite{schwarz1978}
approximates the log marginal likelihood and is \emph{consistent} (picks the true model as $n\to\infty$ if it is
among the candidates).
\begin{example}[Degree selection]
	Data from a cubic plus noise, $n=60$ ($\ln n=\gv{c13_eval/ln_n}$); Gaussian likelihood with $\hat\sigma^2=\RSS/n$,
	$k=$ degree $+2$:
	\begin{center}
		\small
		\begin{tabular}{rcccc}
			\toprule
			degree & $k$ & $\ln\hat L$ & AIC & BIC \\ \midrule
			\gv{c13_eval/ic_rows}
			\bottomrule
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	Both choose degree $\gv{c13_eval/aic_best}$; past it, $\ln\hat L$ creeps up but not by enough to pay for
	the extra parameters, and BIC's penalty grows faster. scikit-learn's \code{GaussianMixture.bic} equals
	$-2\ln\hat L+k\ln n$ with $k=$ means $+$ covariances $+$ free weights, as the script checks.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
	\trap ``Lower BIC means a more accurate model.'' BIC compares models \emph{fitted to the same data} by a
	likelihood-based trade-off; it is not an accuracy, and comparing BIC across different datasets or
	different likelihood definitions is meaningless. ``AIC and BIC need a validation set'' --- no, that is their
	point.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Search and nested CV}
\vocab{Grid search} tries every combination; \vocab{random search} samples configurations and, with the same
budget, explores more values of the parameters that matter; \vocab{Bayesian optimisation} fits a surrogate of
score versus hyper-parameters and picks promising points; successive halving / Hyperband stop bad
configurations early. Whatever the search, its best CV score is optimistically biased (the selection-optimism of
\Cref{ch:setup}). \vocab{Nested CV} wraps the whole search in an outer CV loop to estimate the performance of
the \emph{procedure}.
\begin{example}[Optimism of the inner score]
	An RBF-SVM tuned over a $6\times6$ grid on 120 noisy rows, averaged over six independent training sets: the
	best inner CV accuracy averages $\gv{c13_eval/gs_best}$, nested CV estimates $\gv{c13_eval/nested}$, and the
	tuned models score $\gv{c13_eval/holdout}$ on 10\,000 fresh rows. Nested CV told the truth; the grid's own
	best score did not.
\end{example}

\section{Calibration}
\prototype{Of the e-mails a model scores at 0.8, are 80\% spam?}
A classifier is \vocab{calibrated} if, among examples given probability $p$, a fraction $p$ are positive. Check
with a \vocab{reliability diagram} (bin predictions, plot observed frequency against mean prediction) or the
\vocab{expected calibration error} $\sum_b\frac{n_b}n\abs{\text{acc}_b-\text{conf}_b}$. Logistic regression is
usually well calibrated; naive Bayes (over-confident, \Cref{ch:generative}), SVM scores, boosted trees and
modern deep nets often are not. Fix on held-out data with \vocab{Platt scaling} (a sigmoid on the scores) or
\vocab{isotonic regression} (a monotone step function; needs more data).

	\label{fig:calibration}
\end{figure}

\begin{moral}
	Pick the metric from the cost of each error, the split from how the model will be deployed, and never let a
	number you report have helped choose the model.
\end{moral}

\section\problemhead

\begin{problem}[Metrics from a confusion matrix]
	\onechili
	A COVID test on 500 people: TP $=45$, FN $=5$, FP $=30$, TN $=420$. Compute accuracy, precision, recall,
	specificity and F1.
	\begin{hint}
		Precision uses the predicted-positive column; recall the actual-positive row.
	\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		Accuracy $\gv{c13_problems/p13a_acc}$, precision $45/75=\gv{c13_problems/p13a_p}$, recall $45/50=
			\gv{c13_problems/p13a_r}$, specificity $420/450=\gv{c13_problems/p13a_s}$, F1 $=\gv{c13_problems/p13a_f1}$.
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[AUC by counting]
	\onechili
	Positives score $0.9, 0.6, 0.4$; negatives score $0.7, 0.3$. Compute the ROC AUC.
	\begin{hint}
		Count the six positive--negative pairs ordered correctly.
	\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		Pairs: $(0.9,0.7)$ $\checkmark$, $(0.9,0.3)$ $\checkmark$, $(0.6,0.7)$ $\times$, $(0.6,0.3)$ $\checkmark$, $(0.4,0.7)$ $\times$, $(0.4,0.3)$ $\checkmark$:
		$4/6=\gv{c13_problems/p13b_auc}$.
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Precision at a new prevalence]
	\twochili
	A classifier has TPR $0.9$ and FPR $0.05$. What is its precision when 10\% of cases are positive? When
	0.5\% are?
	\begin{hint}
		Precision $=\frac{\text{TPR}\cdot\pi}{\text{TPR}\cdot\pi+\text{FPR}(1-\pi)}$.
	\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		$\pi=0.1$: $0.09/(0.09+0.045)=\gv{c13_problems/p13c_1}$. $\pi=0.005$: $0.0045/(0.0045+0.04975)=
			\gv{c13_problems/p13c_2}$. Same ROC point, precision collapses: base rates matter.
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[BIC comparison]
	\twochili
	Two models on $n=100$ points: model A has 3 parameters and $\ln\hat L=-120$; model B has 6 parameters and
	$\ln\hat L=-114$. Which does AIC prefer? BIC?
	\begin{hint}
		$\ln100\approx4.605$.
	\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		AIC: A $=246$, B $=240$: B. BIC: A $=\gv{c13_problems/p13d_ba}$, B $=\gv{c13_problems/p13d_bb}$: A. Six
		units of log-likelihood buy three extra parameters under AIC's penalty of 2 each, but not under BIC's
		$\ln n\approx4.6$ each.
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Spot the leak]
	\twochili
	A churn model uses: tenure, plan type, number of support calls in the last 30 days, ``account closure
	reason'', average monthly bill, and the customer's churn probability from last quarter's model. Train/test
	split is random over customer-months. List the problems.
	\begin{hint}
		Ask, for each feature, when it becomes known; then ask whether rows are independent.
	\end{hint}
	\begin{sol}
		``Account closure reason'' exists only after churn: target leakage. Last quarter's model score may encode the
		label if that model was trained on overlapping outcomes. Support calls in the last 30 days must be computed
		strictly before the prediction date. Random splits over customer-months put the same customer in train and
		test (group leakage) and mix future months into training (temporal leakage): split by time, and evaluate on
		customers or months not seen in training.
	\end{sol}
\end{problem}
